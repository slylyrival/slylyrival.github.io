# Probably shrieky and flappy

I've wanted to start digging more into the study of consciousness recently. After a few years of shifts in my views due to some generic mindfulness practices, it seems appropriate to give the topic a little more serious interest. I thought the best place to start would be with Thomas Nagel's 1974 paper "What is it like to be a bat?" (WIILTBAB) which is still referenced often at the beginning of discussions of consciousness, at least to help define the term.

In WIILTBAB, Thomas Nagel presents the case for why consciousness "makes the mind-body problem really intractable." He argues that reductive materialist accounts of consciousness, i.e., explanations of consciousness in the form of physical processes, at present fail to get anywhere close to a successful accounting of consciousness because the tools of  science move us away from subjective sensory experiences, which in the case of consciousness is exactly the object of study.

Nagel first defines a conscious organism as one in which there is something it is like to be the organism. A conscious organism has a subjective experience of the world it exists within. For many conscious creatures in the world, we cannot know what that subjective experience is like. Nagel uses the example of a bat because it is a nice medium distance between creatures very similar to us (apes) and potentially conscious beings very far from us (fruit fly). It seems that to map the world around us through echolocation must be a wildly different subjective experience that the one humans have. Yet, we have no reason to doubt it exists. So there is something that it is like to be bat that is different from what it is like to be human, and we seemingly cannot know what it is. It is tempting, then, to try to study it. 

In the normal course of human scientific study, scientists use various instruments and measures to explore the physical phenomena in the world. In doing so, they move away from subjective experience towards objective explanations. For Nagel, writing in 1974, this meant that we were far, far away from being able to provide reductive materialist accounts of consciousness until some way to describe subjective experiences objectively is discovered.

To demonstrate how typical scientific study of physical phenomena works, Nagel uses the example of lightning. One person over here sees a wicked fork in the night sky and instant shock of thunder, another several miles further away sees a broad flash and rumble seconds later. These are sensory, subjective experiences bound completely to their points-of-view. A scientific study seeks to unite these two experiences in the single objective fact of an electrostatic discharge caused by charge separation in a storm cloud. Scientific study moves from the subjective towards the objective. 

It is extremely difficult to imagine, unless you reject the idea of consciousness as a real experience with a point-of-view, how we could hope to achieve a scientific explanation of these subjective experiences. I'd like to note at this point that I see very little distance between Nagel's claim in *What is it like to be a bat?* and David Chalmers' description of the hard problem of consciousness in 1995, and yet Chalmers seems to get most of the credit (he does cite Nagel heavily in the 1995 paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness.") 

# Welcome to the grand illusion

And so we have to either accept the hard problem for now, for perhaps decades or centuries, or we could simply explain the problem away: enter *illusionism*. To study a bit of the counterpoint to Nagel I've read Chapter 14.2 of Dennett's *Consciousness Explained* where he directly critiques Nagel's paper. I haven't read the bulk of the book, so I can only give low confidence to my explanation of Dennett's argument here, but I basically take him to say that, contrary to Nagel's assertion that we cannot know what it is like to be a bat, we do know much of what it is like. If somebody were to say that a bat perceived an eagle 50 yards away, we could correct them and say no, bats have a much shorter range for their echolocation.

He uses an analogy of imagining what it was like for Leipziger's to hear Bach for the first time in the 17th century. We know they very likely had subjective experiences similar to modern humans. We can research their culture, what they ate, how they dressed, what the architecture was like, and in doing so get a very close understanding to what they experienced. 

Nagel anticipates this response, and in fact says that "in so far as I can imagine [what it would be like to have various perceptual abilities and limits of a bat], it tells me only what it would be like for *me* to behave as a bat behaves," but not "what it is like for a *bat* to be a bat." (I went back and re-wrote the title of the paper to "*How is it for a bat to be a bat?*" after I read that - less snappy, clearer to his point.) Dennett rejects the notion that there is a subjective POV to be found in either bats or humans; there is no "Audience in the Cartesian Theater." He instead encourages the reader to follow his Multiple Drafts theory of consciousness where there is no conscious subjective experience but rather just the brain processing its memories and new input data continuously.

I admit I am only including Dennett's position here for a matter of completeness, to hint at the other side of Nagel's position. I owe Dennett's model much more study before rejecting it completely, but I don't see how the Multiple Drafts model does anything to remove my subjective experience from the equation. 

# Future studies

Nagel closes WIILTBAB by calling for research into the development of an "objective phenomenology," a method of capturing subjective experiences in a way that is objectively comprehensible to beings of similar consciousness. Such an objective methodology may be a step on the road to actually being able to scientifically study subjective experiences. It's been over 50 years since he called for this, so I'm eager to read something more recent to see where the field is at. 

It may be also be worth deciding sooner rather than later whether or not qualia exist, so I don't end up studying something I'm not even experiencing.

Below is an argument outline I've made of WIILTBAB:

- I. Introduction:  Consciousness makes the mind-body problem intractable
	- A. Consciousness = what makes the mind-body problem uniquely difficult
	- B. Consciousness means that there is something that is like for an organism to be that organism
	- C. Functional/behavioral accounts are compatible with absence of experience (e.g., robots)
	- D. Physical explanations -> objective (POV-independent)
		- 1. But phenomenological features are essential tied to a POV
		- 2. Ergo physical explanation cannot capture subjective character
- II. Consciousness = subjective character of experience ("what it is like to be a bat")
	- A. An organism is conscious iff there is something it is like to be that organism
	- B. Epistemic limitation =/= absence of fact
		- 1. There exist facts inaccessible to human concepts
		- 2. These facts are essentially tied to specific points of view
	- C. Bat example:
		- 1. We cannot imagine bat experience using human concepts
		- 2. Imagination yields only "what it is like for us (humans) to behave like a bat"
		- 3. Ergo subjective character is not reducible to objective or behavioral description
- III. Reduction / Objectivity Argument
	- A. A reductive, materialist account of consciousness may still be correct
	- B. Reduction works by identifying an objective reality underlying multiple subjective appearances
		- 1. Example: lightning -> objective electrical process, subjectively appears as visual strike or auditory boom
		- 2. Different observers (e.g., Martians) can access same objective reality
	- C. Increasing objectivity = removing dependence on any specific POV
	- D. Consciousness is essentially tied to a POV
	- E. Ergo moving toward objectivity removes what must be explained
	- F. Ergo standard reduction model fails for consciousness
- IV. Implications for Physicalism
	- A. Physicalism may be true, but we lack any conception of  how it could be true
		- 1. Identity claim ("mental = physical") lacks explanatory framework
		- 2. No theory explains how subjective and physical descriptions converge
	- B. Proposal: develop an "objective phenomenology"
		- 1. Describe subjective experience without relying on shared POV
		- 2. Could partially bridge subjective/objective gap
